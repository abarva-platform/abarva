VENDOR SLA COMPLIANCE RATES BY CATEGORY

RCM Outsourcers (Healthcare Revenue Cycle Management):
  Average SLA compliance rate: 72% — lowest category across all vendor types
  Common SLA metrics: denial rate targets, clean claim rate, AR days, prior auth turnaround
  Most frequent breach: clean claim rate (target typically 95-97%, actual often 88-93%)
  Breach detection time: average 4.8 quarters (15+ months) before client detects systematic breach
  Reason for low compliance: RCM outsourcers are measured against self-reported metrics; independent verification rare
  Financial impact of breach: each 1% below clean claim target = $0.8-2.4M in annual revenue leakage
  AbarVa benchmark: clients who switch from RCM outsourcer self-reporting to independent measurement detect 38% more breaches

EHR Implementation Partners (Epic, Oracle Health, Cerner):
  Average SLA compliance rate: 74%
  Common SLA metrics: go-live date, ticket resolution time, system uptime, training completion
  Most frequent breach: ticket resolution time (critical issues beyond SLA in 34% of cases)
  Specific issue: change requests that block AI/reporting functionality often deprioritised
  Financial impact: Epic configuration blockers can delay AI programme by 3-9 months ($0.8-2.8M per quarter)
  Warning pattern: vendors who propose moving SLA metrics from business outcomes to technical availability

AI/ML Platform Vendors (Databricks, Azure AI, AWS SageMaker, Snowflake):
  Average SLA compliance rate: 78%
  Common SLA metrics: platform uptime, query performance, support response time
  Key caveat: platform uptime SLA (99.9%) does not cover: data quality issues, model performance degradation, feature availability
  Most frequent gap: performance SLA (query time) met but business outcome SLA absent
  AbarVa recommendation: AI platform contracts must include data freshness SLA, model serving latency SLA, and feature availability SLA — not just uptime

Data Management Vendors (Collibra, Alation, Informatica, MuleSoft):
  Average SLA compliance rate: 76%
  Common SLA metrics: platform availability, data catalogue refresh latency, integration performance
  Key risk: data catalogue SLA rarely includes data discovery accuracy (completeness of what is catalogued)
  AbarVa finding: 44% of enterprise data catalogues have less than 60% coverage despite SLA compliance on uptime

IT Infrastructure Vendors:
  Average SLA compliance rate: 81%
  Most reliable vendor category for technical SLA adherence
  Key risk: SLA covers technical uptime, not business impact — infrastructure "green" while AI models fail due to data pipeline issues

Cloud Providers (AWS, Azure, GCP):
  Published SLA compliance: 99.8%+
  Important caveat: cloud provider SLA definitions exclude: scheduled maintenance, force majeure, issues caused by client configuration
  Real-world availability experienced by enterprises: 99.2-99.7% (lower than published SLA)
  Key insight: cloud providers rarely breach published SLA, but published SLA does not capture the business impact of actual incidents
  AbarVa recommendation: measure actual AI system availability (model serving, data pipeline, inference endpoint) rather than relying on cloud provider SLA


SLA ENFORCEMENT PATTERNS

THE NON-ENFORCEMENT EPIDEMIC:
Clients that have ever enforced SLA penalty clauses: 26%
Clients that never enforce despite documented breach: 74%
Average breach duration before any client action: 6.2 quarters (18+ months)
Average breach duration at time of first detection: 4.8 quarters (15 months)

The gap between detection (4.8 quarters) and action (6.2 quarters) represents 1.4 additional quarters of inaction after the client is aware of the breach. This pattern occurs because enforcement authority is ambiguous — operations teams know about the breach but lack authority to invoke penalties; legal and procurement teams have authority but are not monitoring operations.

PRIMARY REASONS FOR NON-ENFORCEMENT:
Reason 1: Structural disconnect (legal/procurement not connected to operations) — 68% of non-enforcement cases
  Operations team observes breach but escalation path to enforcement is unclear
  Legal team has no operational monitoring; procurement focuses on contract renewal, not enforcement
  Fix: appoint a named vendor performance manager with explicit enforcement authority

Reason 2: "Relationship risk" perception — 48% of non-enforcement cases
  Client fears that penalty invocation will damage vendor relationship and reduce service quality further
  AbarVa data disproves this: enforced SLA penalties improve service quality in 72% of cases; inaction degrades it (34% quality decline after first unenforced breach)
  Fix: reframe SLA enforcement as quality management, not conflict

Reason 3: Penalty invocation requires multi-level approval — 38% of non-enforcement cases
  Invoking a $200K penalty requires CFO sign-off, legal review, and board notification
  Approval cycle time: 6-14 weeks — by which time the quarter has turned
  Fix: pre-authorise operations VP or vendor manager to invoke penalties up to defined threshold without escalation

Reason 4: SLA penalties too small to motivate action — 28% of cases
  Penalty is capped at 5-10% of monthly contract value — insufficient to motivate vendor
  Calculation: 10% of $200K/month = $20K penalty — less than the cost of the resources to document and invoke it
  Fix: minimum penalty 15% of monthly value per breach; ratchet provisions for repeat breach

HIGH-RISK SLA PATTERNS TO MONITOR

Pattern 1 — DASHBOARD REPORTING DISCONNECT:
  Description: Vendor reports "green" status on all dashboard metrics while operational experience is consistently red
  Detection: cross-reference vendor SLA dashboard against independent operational measurements
  Frequency: 44% of major IT vendors provide dashboards that do not capture real operational experience
  Example: network uptime 99.9% (green) while AI inference latency 4.2 seconds vs. 0.8-second SLA (red, not measured)

Pattern 2 — CAPPED PENALTIES:
  Description: SLA penalties capped at small percentage of contract value (3-10%)
  Effect: vendor can breach SLA continuously with no material financial consequence — penalty is priced into contract
  Fix: uncapped penalties or penalties that scale with breach severity and duration
  Industry benchmark for meaningful penalty: minimum 15% of monthly contract value per substantive breach

Pattern 3 — TECHNICAL vs. BUSINESS SLA MISMATCH:
  Description: SLA measured on technical metrics (uptime, CPU) while business impact (claim accuracy, model performance, transaction throughput) is unmeasured
  Effect: vendor technically compliant while business outcomes degrade
  Fix: every vendor contract must include at least one business-outcome SLA metric alongside technical SLA

Pattern 4 — VENDOR SELF-REPORTING WITHOUT INDEPENDENT VERIFICATION:
  Description: vendor submits monthly compliance report; no independent measurement
  Prevalence: 78% of IT vendor contracts have no independent verification provision
  Risk: vendor has both incentive and opportunity to report optimistically
  Fix: require third-party SLA monitoring for all contracts >$1M annually

Pattern 5 — MULTI-LEVEL PENALTY APPROVAL (STRUCTURAL KILL):
  Description: penalty invocation requires approvals from legal, CFO, and COO
  Effect: enforcement effectively impossible in practice — approval cost exceeds penalty value
  Fix: pre-authorised penalty schedule with single-level sign-off for invoices below defined threshold


VENDOR CONTRACT BEST PRACTICES (ABARVA FRAMEWORK)

AbarVa recommends the following provisions in all IT and AI vendor contracts:

1. BUSINESS OUTCOME SLA:
  Tie penalties to business metrics: denial rate, fill rate, model accuracy, system availability as experienced by business users
  Technical SLA (uptime) is insufficient — must be paired with business outcome
  Example language: "Vendor shall maintain a clean claim rate of [X]% as measured by Epic Clarity pull; penalties of $[Y] per 0.1% below target per month"

2. AUTOMATIC PENALTY TRIGGER:
  Penalties trigger automatically based on metric thresholds without client action required
  Invoice deduction (auto-deduct from next invoice) rather than separate penalty invocation
  Eliminates enforcement inertia — vendor is held accountable automatically
  Client retains right to waive in specific circumstances, but default is automatic

3. INDEPENDENT THIRD-PARTY MEASUREMENT:
  Named third-party (AbarVa or agreed measurement firm) measures SLA performance quarterly
  Vendor dashboard data cannot be sole basis for SLA compliance assessment
  Measurement methodology agreed in contract, not post-negotiation
  Cost shared: vendor pays 50% of measurement cost (aligns incentives)

4. QUARTERLY BUSINESS REVIEW (QBR) WITH CXO ATTENDANCE:
  Mandatory QBR with CXO-level attendance from both vendor and client
  Agenda: prior quarter SLA performance, current quarter trajectory, open issues with resolution dates
  Minutes document SLA status — creates paper trail for enforcement if needed
  QBR failure to attend by vendor constitutes breach (documented)

5. RATCHET CLAUSES:
  SLA targets tighten by 5-10% per year over contract term
  Incentivises continuous improvement rather than settling at threshold
  Year 1: clean claim 95% → Year 2: 96% → Year 3: 97%
  Vendor pricing reflects improving target in original negotiation

6. EXIT CLARITY AND DATA PORTABILITY:
  All contracts must specify: data ownership, export format, transition assistance period
  Transition assistance: minimum 12 months post-contract at vendor expense
  Data format: open standard (Parquet, JSON, CSV) — no proprietary lock-in
  No exit penalty clauses for underperformance-triggered exits

7. AI-SPECIFIC PROVISIONS:
  Model versioning: vendor must provide model version history and rollback capability
  Explainability: for models in regulated settings (healthcare, FS), explainability report required quarterly
  Drift monitoring: vendor responsible for detecting and reporting model accuracy drift >5%
  Retraining schedule: mandatory model retraining schedule agreed in contract


OUTCOME-BASED CONTRACT BENCHMARKS

AbarVa strongly advocates for outcome-based contracts with AI vendors. These are contracts where vendor compensation is partially contingent on achieving defined business outcomes.

Clients with outcome-based contracts: 22% (still minority but growing)
SLA compliance with outcome-based contracts: 84%
SLA compliance with traditional fixed-fee contracts: 61%
Delta: 23 percentage points — outcome alignment dramatically improves vendor performance

Vendor willingness to sign outcome-based contracts:
  Will negotiate outcome-based: 38% of vendors
  Resist or decline: 62% of vendors
  Key signal: vendor resistance to outcome-based contract is a confidence gap indicator — the vendor knows their product is unlikely to achieve the outcome claimed
  AbarVa recommendation: if vendor refuses outcome-based structure entirely, this is a material red flag

Structure of AbarVa-recommended outcome contracts:
  Base fee: 70-80% of total contract value (covers vendor costs and baseline margin)
  Performance fee: 20-30% tied to achieving defined outcome milestones
  Milestone examples: 90-day (preliminary metric movement), 12-month (target achievement), 24-month (sustained performance)
  Clawback: if outcome degrades after initial achievement, vendor returns a portion of performance fee
  AbarVa takes outcome-based risk alongside clients — AbarVa's own fee structure includes this provision

AbarVa has signed outcome-based contracts in: RCM (denial rate targets), demand forecasting (accuracy targets), fraud detection (loss reduction targets), and data platform (self-service adoption targets).


VENDOR LOCK-IN RISK ASSESSMENT

Lock-in risk is the hidden cost of vendor contracts that restricts the client's ability to switch vendors or renegotiate without disproportionate cost or disruption.

Single vendor for multiple critical AI systems:
  Adds 30-40% to 5-year total cost of ownership (AbarVa benchmark)
  Removes negotiating leverage — vendor knows client cannot switch
  Creates single point of failure for AI programme

No alternative vendor evaluated in 3+ years:
  Cost premium vs. current market: 18-28% (market has moved while client stayed)
  Switching cost unknown (never assessed): clients who have never assessed switching cost underestimate it by 2.4x

Proprietary data format with no export standard:
  Switching cost multiplier: 2.4x vs. open-format vendor
  Data migration risk: significant — data may be partially lost or require extensive cleansing
  Regulatory risk: for healthcare (HIPAA) and FS, data portability obligations exist regardless of vendor contract

Vendor with > 30% of total IT spend:
  Governance risk: board should be aware; concentration risk to AI programme and operations
  Negotiating leverage: limited — client dependency makes assertive renegotiation difficult
  Recommended board disclosure: any single vendor representing > 30% of IT spend

AI vendor with proprietary model (no explainability):
  Regulatory risk: FS (SR 11-7 model risk guidance), healthcare (CMS emerging AI standards)
  Auditability: cannot defend model decisions to regulators without explainability
  AbarVa recommendation: require model card and quarterly explainability report for all production AI models in regulated environments

VENDOR PERFORMANCE TREND MONITORING:
  AbarVa Vendor Intelligence tracks performance trends, not point-in-time measurements
  Declining trend over 3 quarters: higher predictive value than single-quarter breach
  Vendors with declining trend score: renegotiate proactively, not reactively
  Vendors with improving trend after enforcement: relationship is salvageable and should be maintained

FINANCIAL SERVICES VENDOR SLA SPECIFICS:

Core banking vendors:
  System uptime: 99.9% contractual; average achieved 99.6% (breach — each 0.1% = 8.7 hours/year)
  Transaction processing latency: < 2 seconds contractual; peak periods 4-8 seconds (breach)

  Data export for AI training: frequently not in SLA; vendors charge extra for data access that client already owns

Trading system vendors:
  Execution latency: sub-100ms contractual; verify with independent measurement (vendor self-reporting unreliable)

SLA ENFORCEMENT PROCESS (ABARVA STANDARD)

Step 1: SLA Audit (2-4 weeks)
  Extract contractual SLA commitments for all major vendors
  Extract operational performance data for same period (from source systems, not vendor dashboards)
  Calculate breach amount: (contractual SLA - actual performance) × penalty rate × breach duration
  Document root cause and pattern (first breach vs. systematic breach)

Step 2: Business Case Quantification
  Financial value of each breach identified
  Aggregate recoverable value across all vendors
  Risk-adjusted: probability of recovery given contract terms and relationship
  Prioritisation: enforce highest-value breaches first

Step 3: Enforcement Preparation
  Confirm authority: who has legal authority to invoke penalties? Document explicitly.
  Pre-notify: send formal notice of breach identification to vendor (triggers response)
  Legal review: confirm penalty mechanism in contract; no ambiguity before enforcement action
  Escalation plan: if vendor disputes breach, what is the resolution mechanism?

Step 4: Enforcement Execution
  Issue formal breach notice with evidence (contract clause, performance data, penalty calculation)
  Vendor response window: 10 business days standard
  Negotiation: 28% of vendors accept penalty; 44% negotiate partial; 28% dispute and require legal process
  Resolution: invoice deduction (preferred) or penalty credit against future services

Step 5: Remediation and Monitoring
  Require remediation plan from vendor within 30 days of enforcement
  Implement independent monitoring to verify SLA compliance going forward
  Quarterly review: include SLA enforcement status in standard QBR
  Escalation trigger: any repeat breach of same metric triggers contract review, not just enforcement
