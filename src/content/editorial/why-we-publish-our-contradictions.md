Most organizations selling AI guidance — consulting firms, platform vendors, framework publishers — operate on an implicit agreement with their audience: we will give you the answer, and the answer will be coherent. Ambiguity is a product defect. Contradiction is something you clean up before publishing.

We disagree with this, and we think disagreeing with it matters.

Research on complex sociotechnical systems — which is what AI programs are — produces contradictory evidence. Not because the researchers were sloppy, but because the evidence is genuinely contradictory. A pattern that reliably improves outcomes in one organizational context reliably degrades them in another. Claiming otherwise is not simplification; it is falsification.

**What the contradictions actually look like.**

Take the tension between centralized AI governance and distributed program autonomy. The evidence for centralized governance is strong: consistent standards, reduced duplication, clearer accountability. The evidence for distributed autonomy is also strong: faster iteration, better context-sensitivity, higher team ownership. Both bodies of evidence are real. Pretending one does not exist — or that the resolution is obvious — produces practitioners who are unprepared for the specific organizational conditions where the recommendation breaks down.

We document 10 of these tensions in the corpus. They are not edge cases. They represent the most common points where AI programs, following advice that is technically correct, produce outcomes that are technically wrong for their situation.

**The confidence-delta scoring system.**

Every contradiction in the corpus carries a confidence-delta score: a measure of how far apart the competing evidence pools are. A low confidence-delta means the evidence is nearly balanced — the choice is highly context-dependent. A high confidence-delta means one position has meaningfully stronger support, even if the alternative cannot be dismissed. This gives practitioners a calibrated signal rather than a binary directive.

The scoring system is not perfect. No scoring system is. But it is honest about the uncertainty in a way that a clean, contradictions-omitted framework cannot be.

**Why vendors hide this.**

The commercial pressure to publish clean answers is real. Complexity does not merchandise well. Practitioners who are under delivery pressure do not want to be told "it depends." Sales processes do not have a slide for "here are the things we are uncertain about."

We understand this pressure. We have chosen to resist it, not because resistance is virtuous in the abstract, but because hiding complexity from practitioners who will encounter it anyway is a form of negligence. If you are running an AI program, you will hit a contradiction. The question is whether you hit it prepared or unprepared.

**The position.**

We believe the only intellectually honest position for a knowledge layer is to surface what the evidence actually says — including when the evidence points in two directions. We believe practitioners deserve the full picture. And we believe that organizations navigating AI programs are better served by calibrated uncertainty than by confident oversimplification.

Publishing our contradictions is not a weakness. It is the product.
