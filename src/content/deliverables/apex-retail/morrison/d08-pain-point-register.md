## Executive summary

The pain-point register catalogues **23 operator-reported frictions** gathered across 18 structured interviews, 4 store walk-alongs, and 6 category-manager shadows conducted between February 5 and February 20, 2026 [E23]. Clustered by theme, three families carry **82% of the weighted recoverable value** the operators identified: supplier dynamics, assortment bloat, and promotion calendar discipline. A fourth cluster — data and reporting latency — ranks lower on recoverable value but appears as a dependency under every other cluster. The register was prioritized on 2026-02-11 with Marcus T. and Diane R., who flagged supplier concentration, assortment bloat, and promotional depth creep as the top three pain points by recoverable value, aligned with the D07 baseline decomposition. The register is the direct input to the D09 root cause analysis and the D11 hypothesis backlog.

## Key facts

- **23 pain points** identified across 18 interviews, 4 store walk-alongs, 6 category-manager shadows [E23]
- **9 high-severity, 11 medium, 3 low** — weighted by operator-reported annual margin impact and feasibility [E24]
- **Top 3 by recoverable value:** supplier concentration ($5-8M/yr), assortment long-tail ($4-6M/yr), promotional depth creep ($3-5M/yr) [E24]
- Every pain point cross-references to at least one D09 root cause and at least one D11 hypothesis

## Voice-of-operator findings

The register was built from the inside out. Rather than start from the baseline decomposition and work backwards, the Morrison team asked operators — category managers, supply chain planners, store operations leads, pricing analysts — to describe the frictions they live with daily. That approach surfaced three pain points that were invisible in the financial baseline but proved to be upstream causes of margin leakage. The clearest example: pricing analysts reported that the promotional calendar was being "negotiated against" by suppliers who timed trade-funds conversations to lock depth commitments before category-level margin data was available [E25]. That single finding reframed the promotional depth problem from "marketing discipline" to "cross-functional sequencing," with implications for the Phase 3 intervention design.

Diane R. chaired the synthesis workshop on 2026-02-11; she described the voice-of-operator pass as "the first time we've heard all three functions describe the same pain from their own vantage point in one room." The composite disciplines of the operator cohort — merchandising, supply chain, pricing, operations — each brought a distinct frame to the same underlying margin problem.

## Supply chain pain points

Six pain points cluster under supply chain. The dominant one — **supplier concentration** — is the single largest-weighted pain in the register. Category managers described the same pattern in five of eight compressing categories: a single incumbent supplier holds 50%+ of category volume, resists consolidated volume-tier recovery, and holds leverage because switching costs on an in-flight private-label spec are significant [E5][E25]. Secondary supply chain pains include contract-manufacturer capacity ceilings (especially in household essentials), inbound freight variability that absorbs any cost win at the landed-unit level, and packaging spec divergence across 3 suppliers that prevents pooled procurement. Pain #6 in this cluster is the one that Diane R. flagged as "strategically poisonous": **supplier-led trade-fund negotiations** that pre-empt Apex's internal promotional calendar [E25]. That pain point alone sets up three of the H9-H11 hypotheses in D11.

## Assortment pain points

Five assortment pain points cluster together. The headline finding — **long-tail SKU bloat** — was the most agreed-upon pain across all operator cohorts. Category managers, store operations, and supply chain planners independently surfaced the same observation: the bottom 40% of owned-brand SKUs by volume contribute 12% of sales, 28% of markdowns, and 38% of returns [E6]. Store operations added a pain the central team had not seen: **planogram compliance fatigue** at the store level, where the SKU density is high enough that stockers are making real-time substitutions that defeat the intent of the planogram. Other pains in this cluster include pack-size proliferation (Pain #9: 4 pack sizes for a product where the top 2 carry 91% of volume), and a subcategory rationalization that was started in 2024 but never completed (Pain #10: "12 orphan SKUs nobody owns").

## Promotion and pricing pain points

Four pain points cluster under promotion and pricing. **Promotional depth creep** is the well-understood one — depth expanded from -18% average markdown in 2024 to -27% in early 2026 [E7]. But the operator interviews surfaced three related pains that redefine the problem. Pain #12: the **promotional calendar has no governance gate** — any category manager can propose a depth event, and approvals route through a single analyst. Pain #13: **competitive price tracking** relies on a biweekly manual scan that misses 30% of in-flight competitor depth events [E25]. Pain #14: **markdown attribution** is muddled — when a SKU goes on promotion and also hits a natural markdown cycle, the closing ledger tracks the deeper of the two but attribution is lost, meaning the team cannot cleanly tell promotional depth creep from markdown acceleration.

## Data and technology pain points

Four pain points cluster under data and tech, and — despite ranking lower on recoverable value — every interviewee in the pricing, merchandising, and supply chain functions surfaced at least one. The dominant pain is **reporting latency**: category margin data closes on a T+14 cycle, meaning promotional depth decisions are being made against two-week-old data [E25]. Pain #16 is the **supplier cost master** — a set of six disconnected systems that make it hard to see landed-unit cost trends in near-real-time. Pain #17 is the **planogram-to-forecast handoff** — demand forecasts use SKU-level data but planograms are updated at category-level, creating a persistent gap. Pain #18: the **promotional post-mortem** is done manually, after the fact, on a cadence that drifts.

## Pain point clustering and severity

The 23 pain points were scored on a two-axis matrix: severity (operator-reported margin impact in bps) and feasibility (12-month recoverability, graded on effort and dependencies) [E24]. Nine pain points landed in the high-severity / high-feasibility quadrant and are the direct input to the D09 root cause analysis. Eleven in the medium band are carried forward as supporting hypotheses or Phase 4 execution details. Three in the low-severity band are captured but not prioritized; one of those three (pack-size proliferation) will be folded into the Phase 3 assortment intervention incidentally, without needing its own workstream. The cluster weighting — 82% of recoverable value in the top three clusters — is what framed the Morrison team's decision to pursue a **three-lever portfolio** rather than a single-lever program.

## Risks and mitigations

- **[Medium]** **Operator voice may overweight recent pain.** The interview window (Feb 5-20) coincides with the fiscal-year-close crunch, which can bias toward visible, near-term pain. Mitigation: baseline decomposition (D07) and benchmark cohort (D10) were used as independent triangulation sources; top three pain clusters are confirmed by all three signals.
- **[Medium]** **Planogram compliance fatigue may reflect store-level variance.** Only 4 store walk-alongs were completed, across 1,200 stores. Mitigation: Phase 3 assortment intervention includes a store-tier segmentation step; planogram pain will be re-tested at scale before Phase 4 execution.
- **[Low]** **Interview pool skewed toward central functions.** Store operations represented 4 of 18 interviews; merchandising and supply chain dominated. Mitigation: Phase 4 change management package (D22) adds structured store-level engagement before execution goes live.
