## Executive summary

Apex's owned-brand margin was benchmarked against a **six-retailer composite cohort** matched on revenue band ($12B-$24B), store format, and owned-brand program maturity. Apex sits in the **3rd quartile on owned-brand gross margin**, with a **180 bps gap to the cohort median** and a **310 bps gap to the top quartile** [E2]. Four of the six peers **expanded** owned-brand margin during the same 24-month window in which Apex compressed 340 bps, eliminating "tough environment" as a defensible frame [E28]. On operational maturity the picture is more mixed: Apex ranks 2nd quartile on unit cost, 4th quartile on SKU density, and 3rd quartile on promotional depth discipline. The transferable-practice review identified three specific practices — volume-tier supplier contracts, tier-2 SKU rationalization, and a margin-gated promotional calendar — that cohort leaders use and Apex does not. All three map directly to Morrison's selected Phase 3 levers.

## Comparable retailers selection

The cohort was assembled through a three-filter methodology. Filter 1 — **revenue band**: retailers with $12B-$24B annual revenue, placing Apex ($18B) near the cohort midpoint. Filter 2 — **store format**: general-merchandise retailers with owned-brand programs spanning packaged goods, household essentials, and fresh categories (formats matching the Apex 1,200-store footprint). Filter 3 — **owned-brand program maturity**: retailers with ≥5 years of owned-brand history and ≥8% of revenue in owned-brand (Apex owned-brand is 11.7% of revenue). The final cohort of six satisfies all three filters. All cohort data is composite — drawn from public filings, industry reports, and anonymized pattern-library data — and no peer is individually identifiable in this deliverable. The cohort definition was ratified by Diane R. on 2026-02-26 [E28].

## Margin expansion trajectories

The most striking cohort finding is the divergence in margin trajectories during the Apex compression window. Peer A (top quartile) **expanded owned-brand margin by 220 bps** through a volume-tier supplier restructuring that began Q2 2024. Peer B (top quartile) **expanded by 180 bps** through SKU rationalization and private-label specification consolidation. Peers C and D (cohort median) **held flat** through modest supplier negotiation and passive assortment pruning. Peer E **compressed by 90 bps** — but notably less than Apex's 340. Peer F (the cohort bottom) **compressed by 180 bps**, still about half of Apex's decline. Apex's 340 bps compression is thus not just behind the cohort leaders; it is a statistical outlier even against the cohort laggard. The window-matched comparison is the single most consequential analytic finding in the deliverable: it converts the problem framing from "industry drift" to "Apex-specific capability gap."

## Operational maturity benchmarks

The maturity benchmark uses a four-metric scorecard: unit cost (bps vs. cohort median), SKU density (SKUs per linear foot of shelf), promotional depth discipline (average depth vs. elasticity-implied optimum), and supplier concentration (top-3 supplier share of COGS). Apex's scorecard reads: unit cost **2nd quartile** (+40 bps vs. median, a decent position given the supplier concentration gap), SKU density **4th quartile** (1.8× cohort median, the worst metric in the comparison), promotional depth **3rd quartile** (-27% average depth vs. -20% cohort median), supplier concentration **4th quartile** (62% top-3 share vs. 48% cohort median). The unit-cost position is a useful counter-signal: Apex's sourcing team is competent in absolute terms; the cost-side margin gap comes from the structural supplier concentration premium, not from negotiation deficiency.

## Category-level competitive analysis

Five of the eight compressing categories were benchmarked at sub-category level against the three cohort peers that disclose comparable category data. In **packaged goods**, Apex's owned-brand margin trails the three-peer median by 240 bps; the gap is concentrated in dry grocery and snacks. In **household essentials**, Apex trails by 190 bps, with the gap concentrated in cleaning and hygiene subcategories. In **dairy ambient**, Apex trails by 110 bps — the smallest gap of the compressing categories — and the cohort median itself is under pressure. In **canned goods**, Apex trails by 170 bps. In **frozen prepared**, Apex is within 50 bps of the cohort median, confirming that the frozen prepared margin performance (-90 bps compression, the smallest among compressing categories) is close to cohort-competitive [E29]. The category-level view tells the intervention design that the supplier and assortment levers should prioritize packaged goods and household essentials, where the gap-to-benchmark is largest.

## Gap-to-benchmark quantification

Converting the cohort comparison to a recovery envelope: at the cohort **median**, Apex would recover 180 bps of margin, translating to **~$38M/yr** on the owned-brand $2.1B revenue base. At the cohort **top quartile**, recovery would total 310 bps, translating to **~$65M/yr**. The Morrison business case (D16) targets a more conservative **$14-22M/yr** at steady state — representing recovery to roughly the 65th percentile of the cohort, not the median, on the first program cycle [E30]. This conservative framing gives the Phase 5 attestation ledger an honest target: the program does not promise peer-leader performance in fourteen months; it promises closure of roughly 40-55% of the gap-to-median, with the residual recovery as a Phase 5 continuation or second-cycle program. The conservative framing is deliberate and was ratified by Marcus T. at the 2026-02-26 benchmark review [E28].

## Transferable practices

The transferable-practice review identified three cohort-leader practices that Apex does not use. **Practice 1 — volume-tier supplier contracts**: two of the top-quartile peers contract with suppliers on a tiered volume structure with annual rebates rather than fixed unit cost, which enables category managers to consolidate volume for better unit economics without renegotiating contract primitives. **Practice 2 — tier-2 SKU rationalization**: the top-quartile peers maintain an explicit SKU tier structure (hero / support / tail) with rationalization triggers baked into quarterly reviews; Apex's review cadence is annual and does not trigger automatically. **Practice 3 — margin-gated promotional calendar**: two of the top-quartile peers require a category margin threshold to be met before a depth event can be approved, which caps depth creep structurally; Apex's calendar has no equivalent gate [E28]. All three practices are directly reflected in the Phase 3 intervention design (D15); the benchmark-to-intervention mapping is one-to-one.

## Risks and mitigations

- **[Medium]** **Composite cohort data is not auditable at peer-company level.** The benchmark is defensible in aggregate but cannot be replicated by an external reviewer against named peers. Mitigation: D10 cites confidence levels; the business case uses the cohort for directional framing, not for point-estimate projections; Phase 5 attestation is measured against the Apex baseline (D07), not against the cohort.
- **[Medium]** **Cohort peers' trajectories may not be replicable at Apex's scale or operating model.** Two of the peers have meaningfully different store footprints (one is heavier on urban stores, one on rural). Mitigation: the transferable-practice review focused on practice mechanics, not outcomes; the three selected practices are general to the owned-brand margin recovery pattern, not peer-specific.
- **[Low]** **Category-level benchmarking covered only 3 of 6 peers.** Sub-category disclosure is uneven across the cohort. Mitigation: the category-level gap estimates use the three-peer median as the benchmark, with the full-cohort aggregate as a sanity check; the two views are within 30 bps of each other in every category.
