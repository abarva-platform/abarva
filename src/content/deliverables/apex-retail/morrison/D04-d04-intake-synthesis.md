## Executive summary

Between **2026-01-15 and 2026-01-26**, the Morrison intake team conducted **12 structured interviews** across Tier-1 sponsors, Tier-2 influencers, and three field operators (two store managers, one category buyer). Synthesis surfaces **four primary tensions**, **six pattern-library anchors** against the `owned-brand-margin-recovery` canonical pattern [E9], **three notable contradictions** between stakeholder framings, and **14 hypotheses** now carried into the Phase 2 hypothesis backlog. The sharpest contradiction — **Marcus (CFO) frames the problem as cost-side; Katherine (CMO) frames it as brand-equity** [E3][E4] — is productive rather than blocking, and the dual-sponsor governance architecture absorbs it by design. This synthesis is the handoff document from framing to diagnosis; Phase 2 diagnostic work pushes against its claims, not its axioms.

## What we heard

The intake covered 12 stakeholders over 18 hours of structured conversation. Interviews followed a five-part protocol: situational framing, cost-to-serve hypothesis, assortment hypothesis, promotional hypothesis, and governance preference. Quotes below are composite-paraphrased from transcripts; exact transcripts are held in the Phase 1 evidence annex.

**Marcus T. · CFO · interview 2026-01-15.** Framing: the margin compression is not a pricing failure. Apex's consumer price index tracked peer medians throughout the compression window. The compression is upstream — cost-to-serve erosion compounded by promotional depth creep [E3]. He expects the diagnostic to confirm that three lever domains matter (supplier, assortment, promo), in roughly equal proportion, and he is prepared to authorize a $6-8M capital envelope if the business case lands within a 14-month payback horizon. He is explicit that he does not want a program that tries to recover margin through pricing action — that path is not available without breaking the banner's value proposition [E10].

**Katherine P. · CMO · interview 2026-01-16.** Framing: owned-brand is a brand-equity asset, not a margin play. Aggressive assortment cuts will signal retreat to the consumer and affect house-brand perception across adjacent categories. She accepts co-sponsorship on the condition that brand-equity decisions are hers, not the committee's [E4]. She is supportive of supplier cost-side work — it has no consumer-facing signal — and cautious on promotional depth changes because promotional depth is the primary trigger that owned-brand shoppers register. Her concern about assortment rationalization is specific: cuts deeper than 15% in any single category will trigger consumer perception drag that takes 2-3 quarters to recover [E11].

**Diane R. · SVP Supply Chain · interview 2026-01-17.** Framing: the operational erosion is real and she has a running register of it. Supplier concentration has crept in the top 3 categories; Tier-1 cost brackets are reachable if volume is consolidated. Assortment has bloated — 1.8x the SKU density of top-decile peers in three of eight categories [E6]. Promotional depth has drifted from around -18% in 2024 to -27% by late 2025 in owned-brand, without a corresponding drift in national-brand [E7]. She has been flagging these trends in internal reviews for 14 months; the Morrison program is the first committee-level response.

**Reese A. · VP Merchandising, Owned Brand · interview 2026-01-17.** Framing from the category-P&L seat: assortment cuts are operationally feasible but politically difficult. Category leads protect their SKU counts because headcount allocations are partly tied to SKU complexity. She supports rationalization and is prepared to sponsor it at category level, but will need Diane's air cover on the first two category rollouts [E12].

**Jon D. · VP Finance, Merchandising · interview 2026-01-18.** Framing on measurement: the primary challenge is baseline integrity, not metric selection. Apex Finance has restated owned-brand margin numbers three times in 30 months because of COGS allocation rule changes. He strongly endorses the baseline-lock and dual-ledger reconciliation architecture. Recommends the 180-bps cohort-median threshold as the gate floor and the 240-bps target as the committee commitment; the 340-bps stretch should be narrative, not business-case.

**Priya S. · VP Store Operations · interview 2026-01-19.** Framing from the store seat: assortment changes absorb into store operations within four weeks for SKU counts under 800 per store; beyond that, planogram rework takes eight weeks and store-labor is tight. She supports store-tier differentiation (urban vs. suburban density) and asks for a 30-day communications runway before any cut goes live in stores.

**Alex W. · VP Supplier Partnerships · interview 2026-01-19.** Framing on suppliers: Q3 is the renegotiation window; Q1 and Q2 are contract-binding. He confirmed that top-3 supplier concentration is around 62% of owned-brand COGS [E5] and that 5 of the top 20 suppliers are single-sourced with meaningful switching friction. He estimates 18-24 renegotiations are reachable in a focused 90-day wave.

**Mo K. · Director, Pricing Analytics · interview 2026-01-20.** Framing on promotional depth: the -27% owned-brand depth is well past the elasticity-modeled point of diminishing returns (approximately -22%); depth cuts above -22% are subsidizing consumer behavior that would have occurred at shallower depth. Promotional discipline is, in his view, the fastest-cycling lever — 2-3 promotional windows and the math appears.

**Lena G. · Director, Brand Strategy · interview 2026-01-21.** Framing: owned-brand perception moves on three inputs — assortment signal, pricing signal, and in-store prominence. A 15% cut in category SKU depth is within consumer tolerance; beyond that, perception drag manifests. She is the interlocutor who will check Katherine's veto arithmetic [E11].

**Sonia E. · VP Consumer Insights · interview 2026-01-22.** Framing: the brand-equity panel runs quarterly; it is possible to run a mid-quarter wave to establish a Phase 1 baseline against which Phase 4 changes can be measured. Panel runs 1,800 respondents and is representative at regional, not store-tier, granularity.

**Field interview · store manager (urban high-density, 2026-01-23).** Framing: owned-brand is a traffic driver for their store, not a margin driver. They would resist cuts that hit visible facings. They accepted that suburban stores might see deeper cuts.

**Field interview · category buyer (household essentials, 2026-01-26).** Framing: the long tail is the waste — bottom 40% of owned-brand household-essentials SKUs contribute ~12% of revenue, drive ~38% of returns [E6]. Cutting the tail is the right call from their seat; the political hurdle is at category-lead level, not buyer level.

## Pattern-library anchors

The `owned-brand-margin-recovery` canonical pattern [E9] contains 8 levers; intake confirmed six anchors that map Morrison to the pattern:

1. **Supplier concentration premium.** Morrison's 62% top-3 concentration [E5] matches the pattern's "high-concentration" archetype, with 6-9% unit-cost premium vs. diversified base.
2. **Assortment bloat on the long tail.** 1.8x peer SKU density in 3 of 8 categories [E6] matches the pattern's "tail-over-indexed" archetype.
3. **Promotional depth creep.** -27% owned-brand depth vs. ~-18% baseline [E7] matches the pattern's "depth-drift" archetype.
4. **Baseline-integrity fragility.** Three FY restatements in 30 months matches the pattern's "measurement discipline required" flag.
5. **Dual-sponsor architecture.** CFO + CMO pairing matches the pattern's "brand-margin tension" archetype where single-sponsor structures have historically deadlocked.
6. **Parallel-track execution recommendation.** Pattern-library evidence shows parallel-track recovers 35-45% faster than sequential when all three lever domains are active.

Two pattern levers are not anchored in Morrison intake: **private-label expansion** (scope-deferred) and **packaging-cost reconfiguration** (not prominent in intake; flagged for Phase 5 consideration).

## Contradictions observed

Three contradictions that the synthesis preserves rather than resolves. Preservation is deliberate — contradictions are the material the Phase 2 diagnostic works on.

**Contradiction 1 — Cost-side vs. brand-side framing.** Marcus sees cost-side erosion; Katherine sees brand-equity exposure [E3][E4]. This is not two views of the same problem; it is two decisions about which levers are load-bearing. The Morrison governance absorbs it via veto domains. Phase 2 must produce evidence for both framings, not a synthesis that flattens them.

**Contradiction 2 — Assortment depth cut tolerance.** Katherine (via Lena) puts the tolerance at 15% per category [E11]. Reese (via field buyers) says the long-tail is 40% and worth cutting substantially more than 15%. This contradiction is numerical and will be resolved by the Phase 2 assortment analysis (D09) and the Phase 4 store-tier differentiation in D22.

**Contradiction 3 — Promotional depth sequencing.** Mo argues that promotional-depth discipline is the fastest-cycling lever and should lead. Marcus's framing implies supplier renegotiation leads. Alex W.'s contract-binding reality says supplier renegotiations can only wave-execute in Q3. The sequencing contradiction will be resolved in D12 Estimation & Execution Roadmap; the Phase 3 working hypothesis is parallel-track with staggered wave starts.

## Hypotheses to test

Phase 1 surfaced 14 hypotheses carried into the D11 Hypothesis Backlog. Summary list; full evidence framing in D11.

1. Supplier concentration > 60% drives a 6-9% unit-cost premium addressable by renegotiation.
2. Long-tail SKUs (bottom 40% of volume) contribute disproportionate markdown and returns.
3. Promotional depth > -22% passes elasticity diminishing returns.
4. SKU-per-linear-foot rationalization yields 12-18% margin-per-LF lift.
5. Store-tier differentiation preserves urban traffic while enabling suburban cuts.
6. Supplier renegotiation at Q3 reset unlocks Tier-1 brackets for 5-7 categories.
7. Consumer-perception index is robust to ≤15% category SKU depth cuts.
8. Parallel-track execution recovers margin 35-45% faster than sequential.
9. Dual-ledger reconciliation prevents attribution disputes in Phase 5.
10. Category-lead resistance to assortment cuts is higher than buyer-level resistance.
11. Urban high-density stores carry 4-6% revenue risk at target reduction levels.
12. Promotional exceptions budgeted at 1.5% of spend cover competitive-response needs.
13. Supplier second-sourcing readiness exists in 4-6 of 20 top suppliers.
14. Packaging-cost reconfiguration is a Phase 5 lever, not Phase 4.

Nine of these are expected to validate in Phase 2 (per Nexus pattern-library priors), three are expected to reject, and two are expected to defer to Phase 5. Actual Phase 2 resolution is logged in D11.

## Risk callouts

- **Composite-paraphrase slippage (tier: Medium).** Transcripts are composite-paraphrased, not verbatim; aggressive paraphrase could drift from speaker intent. **Mitigation:** each interviewee reviewed their paraphrased quotes within 48 hours of interview; two corrections applied to Alex W.'s framing on single-source suppliers.
- **Intake bias toward corporate roster (tier: Low).** Nine of 12 interviews were corporate; three were field. Store-tier variance may be under-represented. **Mitigation:** Phase 2 store-tier analysis in D09 RCA adds 8-12 additional store-level data points; D22 Change Management allocates additional field listening.
- **Pattern-anchor over-mapping (tier: Low).** Forcing Morrison into pattern-library archetypes risks missing Apex-specific dynamics. **Mitigation:** six anchors are positive matches; two pattern levers are explicitly not anchored. The pattern is a scaffolding, not a template.
